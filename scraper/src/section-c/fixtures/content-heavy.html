Why sitemaps still matter

A sitemap is the cheapest promise a website can make. It says: here is every page I would like you to know about, here is when each one last changed, and here is roughly how important I think each one is. None of that is binding, and no crawler is obliged to believe a word of it, but the file costs almost nothing to generate and it removes an entire class of guesswork from the other side of the connection.

The argument against sitemaps usually runs like this: a well linked site does not need one, because any crawler that can follow links will reach every page eventually. That is true in the same way it is true that a well organised house does not need labels on its boxes. It holds right up until the moment something is stored somewhere unexpected, and then the cost of the missing label is paid over and over by everyone who has to search the attic.

Pages that are reachable only through a search form are the obvious case. So are pages linked from a carousel that renders after JavaScript runs, pages that sit four clicks deep behind pagination, and pages whose only inbound link lives on a page that happens to be slow that day. A crawler working on a budget will simply stop before it gets there, and nothing about that decision is visible to you.

The freshness signal matters more than the completeness one. A crawler that visited last week and sees no changed timestamps can skip the whole site cheaply and spend its budget somewhere it will find something new. That is a service you are doing for yourself as much as for the crawler, because the alternative is a full re-crawl that costs you bandwidth and earns you nothing.

The failure mode worth avoiding is a sitemap that lies. Timestamps that update on every deploy regardless of whether the content changed teach a crawler that your freshness signal is noise, and once it has learned that, it will ignore the signal permanently. A sitemap that is mostly right and occasionally stale is far better than one that is confidently wrong every single day.
